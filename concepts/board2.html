Four directions a customer would describe to a friend

The first set was well made but familiar. Each of these is built on one idea taken from Japanese car culture, used accurately, that no other dealer has. The test for each: could a buyer say what the site was in one sentence the next day?

  1. F
    The plate“The one where every car has a Japanese number plate.”
  2. G
    The catalogue“It looks like the brochure the car had when it was new in Japan.”
  3. H
    The arrivals board“The stock is a station board that flips when a car lands.”
  4. I
    The line map“The cars are stops on a Tokyo rail map.”

All cars, prices and figures are from tokyoprestige.com.au, 11 Nov 2026. Their own photos. Mock-ups of two screens each.

Direction F · mock-up

The plate

Every car wears a Japanese-style number plate carrying its stock number. White with green characters for cars, yellow with black for kei cars and trucks, exactly as in Japan. Cars sit in painted bays, like the yard.

What a customer remembers

The number. “I rang about five-twelve.” The plate is on the photo, the card, the car page and the enquiry, so a buyer always knows which car they mean, and so does the dealer.

Why it is theirs

Tokyo Prestige already gives every car a stock number; this makes it the brand. The same plate can be printed for the windscreen of the real car, so the yard and the site match.

Colour

Type

Barlow Condensed, Barlow.

Homepage, desktop

Strengths

  • An idea that leaves the screen: windscreen cards, social posts, signage
  • Useful, not decoration: it fixes “which car?” on the phone
  • Light, friendly, suits kei trucks and a GT-R alike

Risks

  • A plate must never be mistaken for a real registration: 東京 is not a real issuing office, and that should be checked
  • Less prestige than A for the top-end cars

Direction G · mock-up

The catalogue

Each car is laid out like the sales brochure it had in a Japanese showroom: cream paper, the model name large in Japanese down the page, a specification table headed 主要諸元, page numbers.

What a customer remembers

The feeling of holding something collectable. Enthusiasts keep these brochures; a Century or a GT-R shown this way looks like the car it was when new, not a used listing.

Why it is theirs

The cars are Japanese-market models that never had an Australian brochure. Only an importer can honestly show them this way.

Colour

Type

Cormorant Garamond, Shippori Mincho, Jost.

Homepage, desktop

Strengths

  • The most beautiful of the set; flatters older and rarer cars
  • Each car page could be saved or printed as its own sheet
  • Quiet and premium without being dark

Risks

  • The most Japanese text of any direction: needs a native speaker to check it
  • One car at a time; slower for a buyer comparing ten Crowns
  • Formal for a $17,888 runabout

Direction H · mock-up

The arrivals board

The stock list is a split-flap board, the kind in a Japanese station. Each car is a line; its status reads In Adelaide or Coming soon, and the flaps turn when a car lands or sells.

What a customer remembers

The motion. Flaps turning is the one animation everybody stops to watch, and it gives buyers a reason to come back: to see what has landed.

Why it is theirs

They have 53 cars coming that today are a list with no price and no way to follow them. Here they are the main event, each with “tell me when it lands”.

Colour

Type

Instrument Sans, IBM Plex Mono.

Homepage, desktop

Strengths

  • Puts the 53 coming-soon cars to work collecting enquiries
  • The densest list: ten cars with every figure on one screen
  • Signature animation that is cheap to run (transform only)

Risks

  • Photos are secondary until a line is chosen
  • Flap text is slower to read than plain text; needs a plain-text mode for screen readers and reduced motion
  • The board must be kept current or it reads as stale

Direction I · mock-up

The line map

The stock drawn as a Tokyo-style rail map. Each make is a coloured line and each car a numbered station. Ride a line to compare like with like; change where two cars do the same job.

What a customer remembers

The map, and their stop: “the Crown is T06 on the Toyota line”. On a phone the list becomes a line of stops with previous, next and change here.

Why it is theirs

It is the most “Tokyo” idea without a single cliché, and it turns comparing similar cars, which buyers do anyway, into the navigation.

Colour

Type

Hanken Grotesk.

Homepage, desktop

Strengths

  • Unlike any car site, and bright where the others are dark
  • “Change here” links a Voxy to a Serena: real help choosing
  • Colour coding carries through cards, pages and signage

Risks

  • The most to learn: some buyers will want a plain list, so one must sit beside it
  • Station numbers change as stock sells, unlike stock numbers
  • Must not imitate a real rail operator’s signage

My pick: F, the plate.

It is the simplest idea, the only one that is useful as well as memorable, and the only one the owner can hold: a plate on the windscreen of every car in the yard that matches the site. It suits a kei truck as well as a GT-R.

Combine it with the built site.

These hooks are not whole-site choices. The plate can go onto direction A tomorrow: on every card, car page and the hero. H's board would make a strong Coming soon page inside A, where the 53 cars most need it.

If one buyer matters most.

G wins the enthusiast and the Century buyer on beauty, and costs speed for someone comparing ten Crowns. I is the boldest and the one most likely to be talked about, and the one most likely to confuse.
